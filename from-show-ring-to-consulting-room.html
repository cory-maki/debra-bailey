What the Show Ring Taught Me About Healing

📅 January 12, 2026
✎ Debra Bailey, Ph.D.
⌚ 5 min read

Long before I sat with patients, I spent my early years on the competitive equestrian circuit, training and showing Arabian horses across the United States and Canada. I did not know it then, but that world was teaching me how to do the work I do now.

People are sometimes surprised by this part of my history. What could Arabian horses possibly have to do with psychotherapy? The honest answer is: nearly everything I most rely on.

The show ring demanded patience, emotional attunement, and a work ethic that could not be shortcut. Those same qualities turned out to define my approach to therapy years later.

You cannot rush trust

A horse does not care how badly you want progress today. Trust is built through consistency — showing up the same way, again and again, until the animal learns that you are safe. People are not so different. I cannot argue anyone into feeling safe; I can only be reliable long enough that safety becomes believable.

This is the same steadiness I describe in my writing on what it means to be truly heard — the quiet, repeated reliability that lets a guarded person begin to open.

Growth — whether in a horse or a human — requires trust, consistency, and care. You cannot rush it, and you cannot fake it.

Attunement over control

Good horsemanship is not domination; it is a conversation. You learn to read the smallest shifts — a flick of the ear, a tightening of the body — and to respond before a small worry becomes a large one. Therapy asks for the same fine attention to what a person is communicating beneath their words.

Emotional attunement, I have come to believe, is less a technique than a discipline of paying attention. It is something you practice for a lifetime and never fully master.

Doing your own work

There is one more lesson the ring taught me, though I did not fully understand it until my years directing crisis work: you cannot ask another being to steady itself if you are not steady yourself. Clinicians, like riders, have to do their own internal work in order to genuinely sit with others in distress.

That belief still guides me. If you are curious about how this thread runs through my practice, you can read more about my background and training, or explore how I think about humor and play in the work of change.

Conclusion

The distance from the show ring to the consulting room is shorter than it looks. Both are, at heart, about the same thing: earning trust, paying close attention, and believing that with patience and care, real change is possible.
