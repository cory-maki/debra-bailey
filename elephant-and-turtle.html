The Elephant and the Turtle: How Humor and Play Move Therapy Forward

📅 March 18, 2026
✎ Debra Bailey, Ph.D.
⌚ 6 min read

People often arrive in therapy expecting solemnity. What surprises them is how much room there is for humor, play, and imagination — and how often those are the very things that move the work forward.

The title of my forthcoming book, The Elephant and the Turtle: A Metaphor for the Work of Psychotherapy and Personality Change, points to two truths I have come to trust. Change can feel enormous, immovable, and slow — the elephant in the room and the turtle’s pace at once. And yet it happens, steadily, when the conditions are right.

What I have learned across more than two decades of practice is that lasting change rarely arrives through force of will. It arrives through relationship, through curiosity, and — more often than people expect — through play.

Why play belongs in serious work

When we play, we loosen the grip of the stories we tell about ourselves. A person who has spent years insisting ‘I am the responsible one, I never fall apart’ can, in a moment of shared laughter, glimpse a self that is softer and more whole. Humor is not a way of avoiding pain; it is one of the ways a mind signals that it feels safe enough to loosen its defenses.

Creativity does something similar. A metaphor, an image, a half-joking exaggeration can carry a feeling that a person cannot yet say plainly. The elephant and the turtle are exactly that kind of shorthand — a way to hold something large and something slow in the same gentle frame.

Humor is not a way of avoiding pain. It is one of the ways a mind signals that it finally feels safe enough to let its guard down.

Change at the pace of trust

The turtle matters as much as the elephant. Personality does not reorganize itself overnight, and I am wary of any approach that promises it will. What I have seen, again and again, is that when people are met without judgment, they begin — slowly — to take risks they could not take before.

This is where my earlier life with horses quietly informs my work. Growth, whether in an animal or a person, requires trust, consistency, and care. You cannot rush it, and you cannot fake it. I write more about that connection in my reflections on what the show ring taught me about healing.

An invitation, not a repair

I do not believe therapy is about fixing people. People are not broken machines. My aim is to help clients rediscover their own capacity for healing and change — often a capacity that has been there all along, waiting for a relationship safe enough to let it show.

Humor, play, and creativity are not decorations on that process. They are, for many people, the doorway into it. If you are curious about how I work, you can learn more about my training and approach.

Conclusion

The elephant and the turtle live together in every course of therapy: the weight of what we carry, and the patient pace at which it shifts. Held with warmth — and, yes, with humor — even the heaviest things can move.
